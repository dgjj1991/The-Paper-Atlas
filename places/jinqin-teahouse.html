Chapter 01

What Is This Place?

Jinqin Old Teahouse (金琴老茶馆) opened on September 8, 2024 in the Fuqin neighbourhood of Chengdu's Jinniu District. From the street, it looks like something that's been here forever: water-bucket-thick wooden pillars, grey brick, black roof tiles, a half-open courtyard letting in the sky. In a city full of teahouses, this one stops people mid-step.

Locals call it "Chengdu's Guanyin Pavilion" — a reference to the legendary century-old teahouse in Pengzhen township. The comparison is earned. But Jinqin has a twist: the teahouse is new; the building is genuinely ancient. The structure is a Qing Dynasty wooden mansion, built in the 1740s in a village in Xichong County, Nanchong — roughly 300 years old — that was relocated to Chengdu piece by piece to save it from demolition when a highway was built through its original site.

It's run jointly by the Chengdu Peking Opera Troupe and the local Fuqin Street Office. Order a bowl of tea. The Peking opera is free.

Chapter 02

The Story

Before Jinqin, this corner of Fuqin held a decrepit open-air teahouse: dark, rundown, plagued by noise complaints from neighbouring residents. In early 2024, a district political advisory committee flagged the problem in an official proposal — and the response was unusually creative.

The Fuqin Street Office provided the land. The Chengdu Peking Opera Troupe invested over ¥2 million to source and reconstruct a genuine piece of old China. They found a Qing Dynasty wooden farmhouse in Xichong County, Nanchong — its owners facing demolition for a new highway — and relocated it to the city, timber by timber, brick by brick. The reconstruction used traditional mortise-and-tenon joinery (榫卯), a technique that requires no nails. "Not a single old pillar was wasted," according to the team.

The floor you walk on — uneven, worn to a quiet shine — is "thousand-foot mud" (千脚泥): the patina of a century of footsteps, now carried inside Chengdu's ring road. The iron stove in the corner burns honeycomb coal and heats a fleet of copper kettles. You're sitting in the middle of a rescue operation.

Key moments

  • ~1740s (Qing Dynasty) — Original mansion built in Jinsi Temple Village, Xichong County, Nanchong.
  • 2023 — Highway construction threatens demolition. Chengdu team begins relocation.
  • Early 2024 — District advisory committee proposes upgrading the old teahouse site. Peking Opera Troupe and Street Office form a partnership.
  • Sep 8, 2024 — Jinqin Old Teahouse officially opens. The building stands again — 300 years old, inside a residential neighbourhood.
  • Ongoing — Regular Peking opera performances Tue–Fri at 15:00. Young troupe members use it as a practice stage. Community regulars use it as a second living room.

Chapter 03

The Space

The teahouse covers roughly 500–600 square metres, divided into front and rear halls by a traditional Sichuan tianjing (天井) — an open courtyard that pulls sky, light, and weather into the building. Sixteen pillars hold up the front hall; the rear hall centres on a raised performance stage draped in red silk.

The Courtyard

Tianjing (天井)

On clear days, sunlight falls through translucent roof tiles in columns. On rainy days, water threads down from the eaves like a curtain and drums on the tiles. Both are worth sitting through.

The Heart of the Place

The Iron Stove

An old tiger stove (老虎灶) burning honeycomb coal, a dented iron chimney disappearing into the roof, and three large copper kettles always on the boil. Rare to find inside a city anymore.

The Floor

Thousand-Foot Mud

Uneven, hand-worn earth floor — polished to a subtle shine by a hundred years of footsteps at its original site. It came with the building. You're standing on accumulated time.

The Seating

Bamboo Chairs & Eight Immortals Tables

Traditional woven-bamboo reclining chairs, square Eight Immortals tables, long-spouted copper teapots on every table, bamboo thermos bottles with endless boiling water.

The Back Hall

Performance Stage

Red silk, polished wood. Chengdu's professional opera performers take this stage three or more times a week. Front-row seats fill up by 14:30.

The Walls

Old Things on Display

Erhu, pipa, and other traditional instruments. Opera costumes. Old photographs. Hand-painted red signs reading "Listen to the qin, taste a life." (听琴声，品人生)

Chapter 04

What's On

The teahouse's tagline is honest: spend a little, sit all day, watch a full performance. The programme is run by the Chengdu Peking Opera Research Institute — real performers, not a tourist show.

  • Peking opera (京剧) — Fixed schedule: Tuesday to Friday, 15:00. Young troupe performers. Repertoire includes The Drunken Concubine (贵妃醉酒), Picking Up the Jade Bracelet (拾玉镯), and others. Includes costume display and audience interaction.
  • Sichuan opera (川剧) — Irregular. Includes face-changing (变脸), fire-spitting (吐火), and money-clapper storytelling (金钱板).
  • Pingshū storytelling (评书) — Traditional oral storytelling, a vanishing art form.
  • "Jingqin Yaji" workshops (京琴雅集) — Introductions to Peking opera: learn the orchid-finger gesture (兰花指), the greeting bow, basic postures. Suitable for children and curious adults.
  • Amateur opera fans (票友) — Regulars sometimes take the stage themselves. The audience applauds or corrects them. This is the living version of the form.

Chapter 05

This is one of the best-value cultural experiences in Chengdu. A bowl of tea covers your seat for the day. The opera is free. The total is usually under ¥30.

Chapter 07

Insider Tips

Six things the tourist version of Chengdu won't tell you.

What locals know

  • 01
    Arrive by 14:30 if you want to watch the opera The 15:00 Peking opera session is free — which means it fills up. Front-row seats directly facing the stage go first. Arrive at least 30 minutes early, order your tea, and settle in. The opera is the real draw; don't miss it by arriving late.
  • 02
    Order the "strange-flavour cold noodles" (怪味凉面) The chef created this dish himself. It doesn't appear on most menus displayed outside — ask for it. It's ¥10 and the flavour is genuinely hard to describe: sour, sweet, numbing, spicy, sesame all at once.
  • 03
    Come on a rainy day Most visitors avoid rain. The regulars know better. When it rains, the crowd thins, the courtyard fills with the sound of water on old tiles, and the light goes grey and cinematic. It's the best version of this place. Bring a book.
  • 04
    Share a table — it's expected Sharing tables (拼座) is standard practice in Chengdu teahouses. Your neighbours may nod, may deal cards, may strike up a conversation. This is not rudeness — it's the whole point. Sit down and you're already a local.
  • 05
    The best light for photos: 10:00–11:00 or 14:00–15:00 The tianjing courtyard catches strong slanted light in the mid-morning and mid-afternoon. The iron stove, copper kettle wall, and stage with its red silk are reliably photogenic at both times. Avoid the flat midday light.
  • 06
    The hot water is always free Your gaiwan comes with a thermos of boiling water. When it runs out, flag down any staff member. Refills are unlimited. There is no pressure to order more or leave. The Chengdu teahouse philosophy: the seat is yours for as long as you want it.
